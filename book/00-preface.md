# Preface: What Is Technecy?

> *"For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword."* — Hebrews 4:12

## A coined word for an old craft

**Technecy** is built from the Greek *technē* — craft, skill, art, the knowledge that lives in a maker's hands. The suffix echoes words like *prophecy*: not a thing possessed but a practice exercised. Technecy, then, is **the craft of the word**: the study and practice of how meaning is made, encoded, transmitted, corrupted, restored, and finally embodied.

That craft is ancient. Long before anyone spoke of bits or bandwidth, scribes were counting the letters of the Torah to catch copying errors, prophets were submitting their messages to verification tests, and poets were folding meaning into structures — parallelism, chiasm, acrostic — engineered to survive translation and time. The Bible is, among other things, the longest-running experiment in faithful information transmission in human history. It is also a book that claims information is not the deepest thing there is: behind every word stands a Speaker.

## Why these three disciplines

This book stands at a three-way intersection, and each road contributes something the others cannot:

- **Biblical theology** supplies the *story and the stakes*: a God who creates by speech, covenants by document, judges by the books, and saves by a Word made flesh. It insists that meaning is personal before it is mathematical.
- **Information science** supplies the *instruments*: precise concepts — signal, noise, channel, redundancy, compression, error correction, protocol — that turn vague talk about "the message of Scripture" into questions you can actually examine.
- **Philosophy** supplies the *discipline of asking*: What is a sign? What makes a word *mean*? What is knowledge, and how does it differ from data? Where does an analogy illuminate, and where does it deceive?

The wager of this book is that each field has been quietly talking about the others all along. Claude Shannon bracketed meaning out of his theory and said so plainly; theology is largely *about* what he bracketed out. The doctrine of Scripture's preservation is, on inspection, a theory of error-corrected channels. And the oldest question in the philosophy of language — do words attach to reality, or only to us? — is asked for the first time in Genesis 2, when God brings the animals to the man *"to see what he would call them."*

## A note on commitments and on analogies

This book is written from a posture that takes Scripture seriously as what it claims to be: the word of God given through human words. Readers who do not share that conviction are welcome here; the connections explored stand or fall on their own interest.

One warning, posted at the trailhead: **an analogy is a bridge, not an identity.** When this book says the Masoretes built checksums or that a covenant resembles a protocol, it is pointing at a real structural correspondence — not claiming that God is a network engineer or that grace compiles. Part of practicing technecy is knowing the load limit of every bridge you cross. Where an analogy strains, the text will say so; where you catch one straining unnoticed, file an issue. This is a living book, and that is an invitation.

## How to read this book

The chapters run in order and build on each other, but every chapter is written to stand alone. Along the way you will meet five recurring elements:

- **Selah** — Hebrew psalm-notation, probably meaning *pause*. Questions to sit with. Don't rush them.
- **Workbench** — exercises in the craft: count something, diagram something, measure something.
- **Closer Look** — collapsible deep-dives for the curious. Click to expand.
- **Checkpoint** — self-check questions with hidden answers.
- **Threads** — links tracing the chapter's ideas through the rest of the book.

A few chapters include small live tools that run in your browser on the published site — measure the entropy of a verse, watch three scribes corrupt and a critic restore a text.

**Selah.** Before beginning: when you hear the phrase *"the word of God,"* what do you picture — a sound, a book, a person? Hold the question; the whole book is an answer to it.
