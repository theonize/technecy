# 4 · Babel: When Protocols Break

> *"And the LORD said, Behold, the people is one, and they have all one language... Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech."* — Genesis 11:6–7
>
> *"The limits of my language mean the limits of my world."* — Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* 5.6

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* Babel and Pentecost as the Bible's two great language events. *Information science:* protocols, interoperability, and translation loss. *Philosophy:* whether meaning survives the crossing between languages.

## One language as shared protocol

The Babel story begins with a network in perfect agreement: *"the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech"* (Genesis 11:1). In modern terms, every node ran the same protocol — and the result was enormous coordination power, which the builders immediately spent on a centralization project: one city, one tower, one name (*"let us make us a name, lest we be scattered"*). The stated motive is explicitly anti-distribution; the scattering they fear is the very thing God had commanded (*"fill the earth"*, Genesis 9:1).

God's intervention is precisely targeted. He does not topple the tower or strike the builders; he breaks the *protocol*. Nobody loses the power of speech — they lose **mutual intelligibility**, which is the only thing a protocol provides. The result is forced decentralization: the single mega-node fragments into a distributed system of peoples. Read this way, Babel is not God smashing human achievement out of jealousy; it is a judgment that converts a dangerous monoculture into diversity — at the cost of permanent translation overhead. Every interpreter hired, every mistranslated treaty, every localization bug is a line item of that overhead.

## What translation costs

Philosophy has long suspected that translation is harder than it looks. W. V. O. Quine argued that translation is systematically *indeterminate*: multiple incompatible translation manuals can fit all the observable evidence. Wittgenstein located meaning in *use* — in forms of life — which travel badly. Anyone who has tried to carry an idiom across languages knows the experience: the words arrive; something else doesn't.

The Bible is soaked in this problem, because the Bible *is* a translated book — and, uniquely among the world's scriptures, seems untroubled by it. The Hebrew Scriptures were rendered into Greek (the Septuagint) two centuries before Christ; the New Testament authors, writing in Greek, freely quote that translation as the word of God. Jesus' own Aramaic teaching reaches us almost entirely in Greek dress. Compare Islam, where the Qur'an is properly the Qur'an only in Arabic, and the contrast is stark: Christianity is, in scholar Lamin Sanneh's phrase, a religion of *translation* — committed from birth to the claim that the message survives re-encoding into new tongues. That is a strong information-theoretic claim: the meaning is not welded to one surface form. ([Chapter 6](06-figures-of-speech-divine-compression.md) examines one stunning piece of engineering behind it: Hebrew poetry's translation-proof parallelism.)

It is not a claim that translation is free. Every translation is a commentary; every rendering chooses. The craft of technecy includes knowing what your translation spent and what it bought.

## Pentecost: Babel answered, not undone

If Babel is the protocol-break, Pentecost (Acts 2) is its redemption — and the engineering details matter. The Spirit descends, the disciples speak, *"and every man heard them speak in his own language"* (Acts 2:6). Note what does **not** happen: the crowd is not given one language back. There is no return to the single protocol of Genesis 11. Instead the one message is *multicast with per-recipient decoding* — Parthians, Medes, Elamites, each hearing in his own tongue.

This is the Bible's settled answer to linguistic diversity: not uniformity restored but **every language addressed**. The vision at the end of the book keeps the diversity intact — *"a great multitude... of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues"* (Revelation 7:9). Babel's judgment becomes a feature: unity is relocated from the protocol layer to the message — and ultimately to the Person — it carries. The church is conceived at Pentecost as an interoperability layer: one body whose members do not have to stop being Parthians and Elamites to join it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>Closer Look — Unicode, Esperanto, and the recurring dream of one tongue</strong></summary>

The dream of reversing Babel keeps being re-engineered. Esperanto tried to do it with a constructed human language; it plateaued. Unicode tried something humbler and succeeded enormously: not one language, but one *encoding* in which every language can be written — unity at the representation layer, diversity preserved at the semantic layer. It is hard to find a better technical parable of Pentecost: the solution that worked did not abolish the languages; it gave them a common way to be carried. Meanwhile, the truly Babel-like projects — every platform that promises one feed, one format, one global square — keep rediscovering why centralized monoculture worried Genesis 11.

</details>

**Selah.**

- The builders' motive was *"lest we be scattered"* — safety through sameness. Where do you reach for uniformity when what's actually needed is interoperability?
- Have you ever been genuinely understood across a language or culture barrier? What carried — and what did it cost the speaker?

## Workbench

Run the Babel experiment. Take a richly idiomatic verse — try Psalm 119:105, *"Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path"* — and pass it through a round-trip translation (any online translator: English → Japanese → Hungarian → English, or your own chain). Compare the result with the original. Classify each change: what survived (the propositional core?), what mutated (idiom, register, rhythm?), what vanished. Then try a line of dense slang or poetry and watch the loss rate climb. You are measuring, informally, which layers of meaning are translation-robust — exactly the layers a message meant for all nations must live in.
