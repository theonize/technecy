# 9 · From Data to Wisdom

> *"Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom: and with all thy getting get understanding."* — Proverbs 4:7
>
> *"Where is the wisdom we have lost in knowledge? Where is the knowledge we have lost in information?"* — T. S. Eliot, choruses from *The Rock* (1934)

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* the wisdom literature's epistemology. *Information science:* the data–information–knowledge–wisdom hierarchy and its limits. *Philosophy:* what knowing is *for*.

## The pyramid and the proverb

Information science sketches a famous ladder — **DIKW**: *data* (raw symbols), *information* (data with meaning), *knowledge* (information integrated and usable), *wisdom* (knowing what to do, and why). The hierarchy is regularly criticized in the literature, and rightly — the rungs blur, and nothing about climbing the first three produces the fourth. But as a map of *what can go missing*, it is excellent. Eliot's lament in the epigraph runs the pyramid downward as elegy: each level can be lost in the abundance of the one below.

The Book of Proverbs has been working this ladder for three thousand years, with a more precise vocabulary than the modern one:

- **Data — observation.** *"Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise"* (Proverbs 6:6). And watch the method made explicit in Proverbs 24:30–32: *"I went by the field of the slothful... and, lo, it was all grown over with thorns... Then I saw, and considered it well: I looked upon it, and received instruction."* Observation, attention, reflection, extracted lesson — an empirical pipeline, stated as such, in an ancient text.
- **Knowledge — da'at**: acquaintance with how things stand; in Hebrew idiom, a knowing that is relational as much as propositional.
- **Understanding — binah**: from the root *bin*, "between" — the power to *discern between*, to draw the line that matters. Classification raised to judgment.
- **Wisdom — chokhmah**: and here is the chapter's hinge — *chokhmah* is not primarily intellectual. It is **skill**.

## Wisdom is craft: the Bezalel surprise

The first person in the Bible whom God is said to fill with his Spirit is not a prophet or a king. It is **Bezalel, a craftsman**: *"I have filled him with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship"* (Exodus 31:3) — the wisdom triad granted for metalwork, stonecutting, and carpentry. The same word *chokhmah* describes the sailors' seamanship (Psalm 107:27) and the weavers' skill (Exodus 35:25–26). Wisdom in Hebrew is *technē* — masterful skill, and in the wisdom books, masterful skill **in the medium of living**: knowing, as a craftsman knows, what the material will bear, where the grain runs, what the tool is for.

This is the deepest root of this book's title. *Technecy* — the craft of the word — is not a metaphor bolted onto Scripture from engineering; Scripture's own word for wisdom *already means craft*. The fool in Proverbs is not stupid; he is *unskilled at being human* — data-rich, often clever, and ruinous to himself, like bad code that compiles.

Hence the famous root: *"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge"* (Proverbs 1:7). In information-architecture terms, this is a claim about the **root of trust**: every verification chain must be anchored in something not itself verified by the chain — a root certificate, trusted to validate all the rest. Proverbs locates that anchor in right relation to the Source of reality. Without it, says the proverb, the whole epistemic stack is built on an unanchored chain — clever, busy, and unfounded. (The serpent's question in Genesis 3 — *"Yea, hath God said?"* — was precisely an attack on the root of trust, before any particular fact was disputed.)

## The weariness of the flesh

The wisdom literature also contains the Bible's information-overload text, startling in its modernity: *"of making many books there is no end; and much study is a weariness of the flesh"* (Ecclesiastes 12:12). Qoheleth wrote it when books were scrolls; the principle has merely been multiplied by twelve orders of magnitude. Herbert Simon gave the modern corollary in 1971: *a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.* Attention, not information, is now the scarce resource — which means the wisdom question has inverted. Proverbs assumed instruction was scarce and attention available (*"wisdom crieth without"* and few listen, Proverbs 1:20). We live amid the opposite: an unending shout of competing instruction, and the skill that must be learned is **selective deafness** — what to refuse to know, what to decline to consider, what feed to leave unread.

Ecclesiastes ends its overload-warning with a compression: *"Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man"* (12:13). After the many books — a summary. After unbounded data — a small, sufficient, executable core. That movement, from sprawl to gist to practice, is the entire DIKW ascent performed in two verses.

Proverbs 8 personifies Wisdom as present at creation: *"When he prepared the heavens, I was there... then I was by him, as one brought up with him"* (8:27–30) — where the debated Hebrew word *amon* is often rendered *master workman*. The creation of Genesis 1 — that great act of dividing, ordering, naming ([Chapter 3](03-naming-the-animals.md)) — is here ascribed a craftsman at the Creator's side. Christian readers from the earliest centuries heard in this passage the Logos of John 1 ([Chapter 1](01-in-the-beginning-was-information.md)): the Word by whom all things were made, wisdom and word being two names of one mediator. The universe, in this picture, is not raw data. It is workmanship.

</details>

**Selah.**

- Eliot's two losses run in order: wisdom lost in knowledge, knowledge lost in information. Which loss is further advanced in you — and what would Qoheleth tell you to stop reading?
- If *chokhmah* is skill, wisdom grows the way skill grows: practice, correction, apprenticeship. Who is your master craftsman? Whose apprentice are you, on purpose?

## Workbench

Run an information-diet audit for one week. Three columns per day: *intake* (what you read/watched/heard, roughly quantified), *retention* (what you can actually recall at day's end), *enactment* (anything that changed a decision or action). Most audits return a pyramid that is shockingly flat at the top — gigabytes in, a sentence retained, nothing enacted. Then perform Qoheleth's move: write the one-sentence "conclusion of the whole matter" your week's intake actually supports, and decide what *not* to read next week. Deletion is a wisdom operation.
