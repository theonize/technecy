# 2 · The Image and the Icon

> *"So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them."* — Genesis 1:27
>
> *"A map is not the territory it represents."* — Alfred Korzybski (1931)

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* the image of God, and why graven images are forbidden. *Information science:* representation — how data structures stand for the world. *Philosophy:* the theory of signs, from Augustine to Peirce.

## A living representation

In the ancient Near East, a king who could not be everywhere set up *images* of himself — statues in conquered cities, declaring whose rule held there. Genesis 1 borrows the political vocabulary and democratizes it scandalously: not the king but *humanity* — male and female, every one — is the image of God, planted in creation to represent the absent-yet-present Sovereign.

Read informationally, the *imago Dei* is a claim about representation. An image carries information *about* its original and acts *on behalf of* it. Human beings are described as God's authorized representation in the medium of creation: living icons with delegated authority (*"let them have dominion"*, Genesis 1:26). This is why the Bible's ethics of human dignity are representational ethics: violence against a person is treated as defacement of God's image (Genesis 9:6), and James forbids cursing men *"which are made after the similitude of God"* (James 3:9). You cannot honor an original while vandalizing its authorized copies.

## Why no graven images

Against this background the second commandment stops looking arbitrary. *"Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image"* (Exodus 20:4) is, in effect, a divine policy on representation: no static, human-made encoding of God is authorized. Why? Deuteronomy 4:15 gives the reason explicitly: *"Ye saw no manner of similitude on the day that the LORD spake unto you... out of the midst of the fire"* — God revealed himself in *words*, not form, so any visual encoding would be an invention, not a copy.

Every engineer knows the problem: a representation captures some features of its original and silently discards the rest. A wooden idol encodes "god" as local, visible, passive, ownable — exactly the attributes the God of Israel does not have. The prophets' satire of idolatry is essentially an information-theoretic critique: the idol-maker burns half the log for dinner and worships the other half (Isaiah 44:14–17), then must carry his god, *"because it cannot go"* (cf. Isaiah 46:1–7). The representation has none of the original in it. **An idol is a lossy encoding mistaken for its source** — a map worshiped as territory.

The New Testament's resolution is not better statuary but a living image: Christ, *"the image (eikōn) of the invisible God"* (Colossians 1:15), and the restoration of the defaced image in human beings (*"renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him"*, Colossians 3:10). The only faithful representation of a living God turns out to be a living one.

## Signs: Augustine to Peirce

Long before semiotics had a name, Augustine defined its core concept in *On Christian Doctrine*: a sign is *a thing which, over and above the impression it makes on the senses, causes something else to come into the mind*. Smoke signifies fire; a track signifies the animal; a word signifies by convention. Augustine divided the world into *things* and *signs* and taught Scripture-reading as the disciplined traversal from sign to thing — arguably the first hermeneutics textbook in the Western tradition.

Charles Sanders Peirce, the founder of modern semiotics ("we think only in signs"), gave the craft its standard toolkit by distinguishing three ways a sign can relate to its object:

- **Icon** — signifies by *resemblance* (a portrait, a diagram, an onomatopoeia).
- **Index** — signifies by *causal or physical connection* (smoke, a fingerprint, a weathervane).
- **Symbol** — signifies by *convention* (almost all words, flags, mathematical notation).

Scripture uses all three with precision. The bronze serpent is an icon (Numbers 21:8–9) — and note what happens to it: generations later Israel begins worshiping it, and Hezekiah smashes it, naming it *Nehushtan*, "a piece of bronze" (2 Kings 18:4). The rainbow is an index of the covenant (Genesis 9:13). The words of the Law are symbols, to be bound on hands and written on doorposts (Deuteronomy 6:8–9). The biblical pattern is consistent: signs are honored as signs and destroyed the moment they are treated as the thing itself.

## Maps, models, and the idols of the model

Korzybski's slogan — *the map is not the territory* — is the secular twin of the second commandment. Every model compresses; that is what makes it useful and what makes it dangerous. A database schema decides which facts about a customer exist; everything else, the system cannot see. A metric stands in for a goal until, inevitably, someone optimizes the metric instead of the goal ([Chapter 7](07-law-as-code-covenant-as-protocol.md) takes this up as the Pharisee problem).

Technecy's discipline here is double: make representations *well* (that is the craft), and refuse to bow to them (that is the commandment). The two skills are one skill. The best engineers and the best theologians share a reflex: they always know what their model leaves out.

**Selah.**

- Your phone holds a representation of you — contacts, photos, locations, purchases. Which of Peirce's three sign-types is it? What does it systematically leave out?
- Hezekiah destroyed a sign that God himself had commanded to be made. When does a good representation's time come to an end?

## Workbench

Take ten minutes with the room you're in. List five things that function as signs. Classify each as icon, index, or symbol — and note that many are mixtures (a photograph is icon by resemblance and index by causation). Then the harder question: find one representation in your daily life that has quietly become a territory — a number, dashboard, or profile you treat as the reality it was meant to summarize.
