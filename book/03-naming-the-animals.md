# 3 · Naming the Animals: The First Ontology

> *"And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof."* — Genesis 2:19
>
> *"The first step of science is to know one thing from another."* — Carl Linnaeus

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* naming as the first delegated human task. *Information science:* ontologies, taxonomies, and knowledge graphs. *Philosophy:* realism vs. nominalism — are categories discovered or invented?

## Creation begins with classification

Before Genesis describes any human work, it describes God's own: and a striking amount of it is *information work*. God **divides** — light from darkness, waters above from waters below, sea from dry land — and God **labels**: *"God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night"* (Genesis 1:5). Separation and naming: any data architect will recognize the workflow. Creation in Genesis 1 is not only the production of stuff but the imposition of distinction upon it — the bootstrapping of an ontology, in both the philosopher's sense (what exists) and the computer scientist's (a formal structure of categories and relations).

Then comes the delegation. The first recorded task given to a human being is to *continue the classification*: God brings the animals to the man *"to see what he would call them."* Note what the verse does **not** say. God does not dictate the names; he watches. And whatever the man calls each creature, *"that was the name thereof"* — the human-assigned labels are ratified as real. The first human act in Scripture is an act of knowledge engineering, performed under authority, with creative freedom, and with consequences that stick.

## Discovered or invented? The oldest debate in philosophy

Plato's *Cratylus* — the first known treatise on names — already stakes out the two camps: do names attach to things by nature (so that a name can be *correct*), or only by convention? Medieval philosophy fought the same battle as **realism vs. nominalism**: do categories like *bird* and *justice* exist in reality, or are they only labels we paste over a world of particulars? The debate is not antique; it is alive every time a data-modeling team argues about whether `Customer` and `Account` are really different entities, and every time biologists fight over whether a species concept is natural or pragmatic.

Genesis 2:19 is a remarkably balanced text to set beside that debate. Against pure nominalism: the animals are real, formed by God, brought in their kinds — the world has joints before the man speaks. Against pure realism: the names genuinely originate with the man — God waits *to see* what he will say. The picture is something like **stewarded realism**: reality has structure we do not invent, but our categorizations of it are genuinely ours — creaturely, revisable, and yet capable of being *true*. Names in this picture are neither arbitrary stickers nor magic essences; they are craftwork, and can be done well or badly.

Hebrew thought loads names heavily. A **shem** (name) carries character and destiny, which is why biblical renamings matter: Abram becomes Abraham when the covenant enlarges (Genesis 17:5), Jacob becomes Israel after the wrestling (Genesis 32:28), Simon becomes Peter with a new commission (John 1:42). A renaming in Scripture is what an engineer would call a schema migration: the underlying entity persists, but its definition — its relations, its role in the larger model — has changed, and the change is significant enough to need a new identifier.

## Ontologies, ancient and modern

Computer science reinvented Adam's job description in the 1990s and called it **ontology engineering**: building formal vocabularies of entities, properties, and relations so that machines (and teams) can share meaning. Knowledge graphs, RDF triples, biomedical ontologies, library classification — all are heirs of the same craft, and all rediscover the same hard lessons: every classification serves a purpose; every boundary case is a theological argument waiting to happen; whoever controls the schema controls what can be said.

Scripture contains worked examples of deliberate, purpose-built classification:

- **Genealogies** — the Bible's original linked data: long chains of *begat* relations whose purpose is not trivia but *traceability*, establishing tribe, inheritance, priesthood, and ultimately messianic lineage (Matthew 1 and Luke 3 are provenance chains for the central claim of the book).
- **The clean/unclean taxonomy** of Leviticus 11 — a full classification system with explicit decision criteria (cloven hoof AND chews cud; fins AND scales) and theological semantics: the categories exist to teach a people to *make distinctions* (*"to make a difference between the unclean and the clean"*, Leviticus 11:47).
- And then, astonishingly, a **schema migration** on that very taxonomy: Peter's vision in Acts 10 (*"What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common"*) deliberately revises the clean/unclean ontology to admit the Gentiles. The categories were real, purposeful — and revisable by their Author.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>Closer Look — what naming is not: dominion vs. domination</strong></summary>

It is sometimes claimed that naming in the ancient world was an act of mastery — to name something is to own it. The text is more delicate. Adam names the animals as part of a search for a counterpart (*"but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him"*, Genesis 2:20) — classification in service of relationship, not control. And when the woman is built, Adam's response is not a label but a poem (Genesis 2:23), the Bible's first recorded human words. The lesson for every knowledge engineer: taxonomy is for the sake of communion, and there are realities that classification alone cannot reach — for those, you need poetry. [Chapter 6](06-figures-of-speech-divine-compression.md) is about why.

- Whoever controls the schema controls what can be said. Where have you seen a category system (in software, in church, in politics) quietly decide an argument before it started?

## Workbench

Build the Genesis 1 ontology. On paper, diagram the entities and relations of the chapter: the three great separations (light/darkness, waters above/below, sea/land), the named categories (Day, Night, Heaven, Earth, Seas), the populating of each domain (lights, birds/fish, animals/humans), and the relations (*rules over*, *fills*, *is image of*). Notice the structure many commentators see: days 1–3 create domains, days 4–6 create their inhabitants — a two-column schema. Then ask: what does this classification exist *for*? (Hint: the chapter's refrain is an evaluation field — *"and God saw that it was good."*)
