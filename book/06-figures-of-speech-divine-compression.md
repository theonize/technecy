# 6 · Figures of Speech: Divine Compression

> *"I have multiplied visions, and used similitudes, by the ministry of the prophets."* — Hosea 12:10
>
> E. W. Bullinger, *Figures of Speech Used in the Bible* (1898), on his subject: a figure is a word or sentence *thrown into a peculiar form* — a deliberate departure from plain usage, never for ornament, but to add force, focus the attention, and mark emphasis.

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* why revelation arrives so often as poetry, image, and parable. *Information science:* compression, encoding density, and channel design. *Philosophy:* metaphor as a way of knowing, not a decoration on knowing.

## Figures are precision instruments

E. W. Bullinger's massive catalog — the work that this repository's [LINKS page](../LINKS.md) has pointed to from the beginning — documents **more than two hundred distinct figures of speech** in Scripture, from *anabasis* to *zeugma*. His central insight deserves restating in modern terms: a figure of speech is not a decoration but a **markup language**. A departure from plain syntax is a flag planted in the text — *emphasis here, attend here* — exactly as a markup tag signals structure that flat text cannot carry. To read a figure "literally" is to read the markup as content: a parsing error, not piety.

This reframes a tired argument. The question is never whether to take Scripture "literally or figuratively," as if figures were a softer grade of truth. Hyperbole, irony, anthropomorphism, and metaphor are *precision* instruments — they say true things that plain prose says worse, or cannot say at all. When the psalmist writes that God *"shall cover thee with his feathers"* (Psalm 91:4), the figure transmits protection, intimacy, and tenderness in six words; the "literal" paraphrase would take a paragraph and lose the warmth — a worse compression ratio *and* lossier.

## A taxonomy of two hundred tools

A sampler from Bullinger's catalog, read with an engineer's eye:

- **Parallelism** — the signature of Hebrew poetry (codified by Robert Lowth, 1753): the second line restates, contrasts, or completes the first. *"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork"* (Psalm 19:1). This is **rhyme at the semantic layer** — and here is the staggering design consequence: because the pattern lives in *meaning* rather than sound, **it survives translation intact**. Rhyme and meter die crossing a language border; parallelism walks through unharmed. A poetry meant for all nations is encoded in the one poetic layer that is translation-proof. ([Chapter 4](04-babel-when-protocols-break.md) is the context for why that matters.)
- **Parallelism is also redundancy** — each thought transmitted twice in different words, which is precisely Shannon's prescription for a noisy channel ([Chapter 5](05-the-canon-an-error-corrected-channel.md)): if a line is corrupted or a nuance lost, its twin preserves the sense.
- **Chiasm** — mirror structure, A-B-C-C′-B′-A′. The compact masterpiece is Genesis 9:6: *"Whoso sheddeth / the blood / of man, // by man / shall his blood / be shed"* — the word order folds back on itself, the form (mirrored retribution) enacting the content (measure for measure). Larger chiasms organize whole chapters; the center of the mirror marks the emphasis.
- **Acrostic** — Psalm 119's twenty-two stanzas walk the Hebrew alphabet, eight verses per letter: an index structure, built for memory addressing — and a completeness claim (everything from aleph to tav about the word of God).
- **Inclusio** — repeating the opening at the close (*"O LORD our Lord, how excellent is thy name"* framing Psalm 8): begin/end delimiters, marking the unit's boundaries for a manuscript culture with no paragraph breaks.
- **Anthropomorphism** — God's "hand," "eyes," "nostrils": deliberately lossy projection of the infinite into human-shaped vocabulary, the only vocabulary the channel supports. The figure is the honest admission of the encoding limit.

## Parables: encoding with an audience filter

Metaphor is compression. Lakoff and Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* showed that abstract thought itself runs on metaphor (*argument is war*; *time is money*; *understanding is seeing*) — metaphors are not paraphrasable into neutral prose because they are the format in which the thinking happens. A good metaphor achieves what engineers dream of: enormous semantic payload, tiny symbol count, and **graceful degradation** — a child decodes the shepherd-and-sheep at one depth, a scholar at another, and neither gets garbage.

Jesus' parables add a stranger property. Asked why he teaches in parables, he gives an answer that has unsettled readers ever since: *"because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not"* (Matthew 13:13) — to those open, more is given; to the hostile, the story stays a story. A parable is a message whose decoding depends on the *state of the receiver*. The same broadcast sorts its own audience: those with *"ears to hear"* carry the key; those without walk away with a tale about seeds. It is selective encoding — not encryption by secrecy of algorithm, but by condition of heart. Revelation and concealment in a single transmission.

<summary><strong>Closer Look — Bullinger's wager: figures as inspiration's fingerprints</strong></summary>

Bullinger's own argument for cataloging figures was theological: if every word of Scripture is God-given, then every *departure from plain form* is deliberate — a divinely placed emphasis marker, never accident or flourish. One need not follow Bullinger in every identification (his catalog is generous) to take the methodological point: in a text you trust, anomaly is signal, not noise. Engineers have the same rule for reading good code: when a master programmer does something odd, you assume intent and look harder. The figure list is thus a reading discipline — a trained refusal to skim past the strange.

- *"The LORD is my shepherd"* — try to restate the whole of Psalm 23 in figure-free prose. What is lost first: information, or the ability to *rest* in the information?
- Parables sort their hearers by receptivity. Has a text ever reopened to you years later — same words, different you? What changed in the receiver?

## Workbench

Take Psalm 23 (six verses) and mark its figures: the governing metaphor (shepherd), the shift of metaphor mid-psalm (host and table), the valley imagery, the anthropomorphic comfort (*rod and staff*), the inclusio of the LORD's presence. Count words in the Hebrew if you can (about fifty-five) or English (about a hundred and twenty). Then estimate honestly: how many words of plain prose would carry the same content — and would it *be* the same content? You are computing a compression ratio for one of the most-decoded messages in human history.
