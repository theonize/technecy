# 12 · Memory, Identity, and Resurrection

> *"It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption... It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body."* — 1 Corinthians 15:42, 44
>
> *"...as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person."* — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.27

**In this chapter** — *Theology:* resurrection hope and the memory of God. *Information science:* pattern identity, copies, backups, and the duplication problem. *Philosophy:* what makes you *you* across time — and across death.

## The Ship of Theseus sails through your body

Plutarch's old puzzle — if every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship? — is not a thought experiment for human beings; it is a medical description. Your matter turns over continuously; most of the atoms that were "you" some years ago have been replaced. Whatever personal identity consists in, it cannot be the persistence of particular stuff.

Locke's classic answer relocated identity from substance to **memory**: you are the same person as far back as your consciousness extends. The objections are also classic — memories fade, distort, and can in principle be false; the amnesiac does not become morally innocent. The modern, computationally flavored answer generalizes Locke: you are a **pattern** — an information structure that persists through changing substrate, the way a wave persists through changing water. The pattern view explains the Ship of Theseus elegantly. And then it runs into the wall every engineer will recognize: **patterns can be copied.** If the pattern is the person, then a perfect copy of the pattern is the person — and if we run two, which one is you? Both? The duplication problem is the cliff-edge of every "mind uploading" proposal: a migration that ends with you *and* your copy in the room has manifestly not transported you; it has published you. A copy of a pattern is a new token of the type ([Chapter 10](10-the-word-made-flesh.md)) — and persons, whatever they are, are not types.

## Not backup, but resurrection

It is tempting — and increasingly common — to imagine resurrection through the data-center metaphor: God keeps a perfect backup and, on the last day, restores from it. The metaphor fails in an instructive direction. A restored backup is a copy — exactly the thing the duplication problem shows is *not* the original person. If resurrection were restoration-from-archive, the risen would be flawless replicas of the dead, and the dead would simply be gone.

Paul's actual image is organic, not archival: *"that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die"* — the **seed** (1 Corinthians 15:36–38). A seed is not a backup of the plant; it is the *same organism* passing through transformation, with both continuity and radical change: *sown in corruption, raised in incorruption*. And the prototype is concrete: the risen Christ is no replica — he is *himself*, recognizably and bodily (*"handle me, and see"*, Luke 24:39), still bearing his wounds (John 20:27) — the scars are the continuity markers, the same body glorified, not a fresh instance with the damage patched out.

Where is identity held across the gap of death, when the body is dust and no creaturely memory of us survives? Scripture's answer is neither substance nor pattern but **relationship to the One who does not forget**. *"A book of remembrance was written before him for them that feared the LORD"* (Malachi 3:16). Jesus, arguing for resurrection from a tense, rests the case on God's present relation to dead patriarchs: *"I am the God of Abraham... God is not the God of the dead, but of the living"* (Matthew 22:32). To be remembered by God is not to be archived by God; God's remembering, throughout Scripture, is *act* — when God "remembers" Noah, Rachel, his covenant, things happen (Genesis 8:1; 30:22). The thief on the cross asks for precisely this and is promised more than recall: *"Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom"* (Luke 23:42). Identity, on this account, is held neither in your atoms nor in your pattern but in the unbroken intention of God toward *you* — and resurrection is that intention enacted: not data restored, but a person raised.

## Forgetting as grace

Here the information age meets a biblical idea it desperately needs. Our systems are built never to forget: logs, archives, caches, screenshots — the internet's default is total retention, and a youthful folly is retrievable forever. Scripture attributes to the omniscient God a stranger power: *"I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more"* (Jeremiah 31:34); *"as far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us"* (Psalm 103:12).

This cannot be data loss — omniscience does not have storage failures. It is **authoritative non-recall**: a sovereign decision that a record will never again be *brought into the relationship* — never retrieved against you, never entered into evidence. Which is, on reflection, what human forgiveness is too: not amnesia (you cannot will amnesia) but a commitment about retrieval — refusing to keep re-litigating what has been released. The legal debates about a "right to be forgotten" are groping toward something Jeremiah 31 had already distinguished: the difference between *what is stored* and *what is held against you*. Grace is a retrieval policy before it is a feeling.

The dead now persist among us as data — accounts, photo streams, message histories; soon, fine-tuned conversational simulacra trained on a lifetime of text. Such an artifact may comfort, the way a photograph comforts. But this chapter's distinctions name precisely what it is: a *pattern-copy* — a token generated from records, not the person held in the memory of God. The danger is docetic ([Chapter 10](10-the-word-made-flesh.md)): mistaking representation for presence, the seeming for the one who seemed. Christian grief has never pretended the dead are not gone; it claims instead that they are *kept* — and that keeping is not ours to engineer. *"Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord... and their works do follow them"* (Revelation 14:13).

**Selah.**

- What in your past do you keep retrieving against yourself — a record God has declared he will not recall? What would adopting his retrieval policy feel like, practically, this week?
- If your identity is finally held in God's unbroken intention toward you rather than in your memories or achievements, what are you free to stop defending?

## Workbench

Two exercises, one technical, one personal. **(1)** Write down your continuity criteria: list what would have to persist for a future being to *be you* — memories? character? body? relationships? this exact consciousness? Now test each against the duplication problem (a perfect copy has it too — so is it sufficient?). Notice which criteria survive. **(2)** The Malachi exercise: the "book of remembrance" was written for those who *"spake often one to another"* about the LORD. Write the entry you would want read at the last day — one paragraph. What does its content tell you about what you believe actually persists?
